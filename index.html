A lab-built copy of the brain's "release growth hormone" signal — with a chemical tail that clings to a blood protein and stretches its life from minutes to about a week. A biotech company studied it in people, then shelved the whole program. Here is the molecule, separated from the forum hype.

Quick read

CJC-1295 with DAC is a synthetic version of GHRH, the brain hormone that tells the pituitary gland to release growth hormone. 01The analog

What CJC-1295 with DAC actually is

CJC-1295 with DAC is two inventions in one: a synthetic copy of the brain's growth-hormone-release signal, plus a chemical hitchhiker's pass that lets it ride the bloodstream for days. Everything sold under this name is a research chemical with no approved use.

The same signal, two lifespans. The DAC tail is the entire difference between the two molecules sold as 'CJC-1295'.

A GHRH analog

A synthetic version of growth-hormone-releasing hormone — the 44-amino-acid signal the hypothalamus uses to tell the pituitary to release growth hormone. CJC-1295 copies the active 29-amino-acid fragment, with small tweaks for stability.

The DAC tail

A maleimide chemical group — the Drug Affinity Complex — attached to the peptide. In the blood it latches onto albumin, the body's long-lived carrier protein, which shields the drug from clearance instead of letting it vanish in minutes.

Not the no-DAC version

CJC-1295 without DAC (often called modified GRF 1-29) is a different, short-acting molecule with a half-life around 30 minutes. Forums constantly mix the two up — and the evidence and risks are not interchangeable.

Plain English

Think of GHRH as the brain's starting pistol for growth hormone. The no-DAC version fires once and vanishes. The DAC version tapes itself to a marathon runner (albumin) and keeps going for days. Same starting pistol — wildly different race.

02The DAC story

The long-acting experiment

In the 2000s a biotech company set out to make growth-hormone therapy more convenient — one shot a week instead of daily. The science worked. The business didn't.

From real drug program to forum staple — the arc of CJC-1295 with DAC.
A real human program

Small studies in people showed single injections raised growth hormone and kept IGF-1 elevated for days. The DAC concept proved itself in humans — the chemistry genuinely worked as designed.

Shelved, not loudly failed

The developer discontinued the program without a dramatic public failure. Long-acting growth-hormone approaches faced commercial and safety hurdles across the industry at the time.

Two molecules, one name

The DAC and no-DAC versions diverged completely in the gray market, and most forum talk about 'CJC' is actually about the short-acting one. Stories about one do not transfer to the other.

Plain English

This is a real drug candidate that got fairly far — human data, working pharmacology — then quietly died in development. The gray market resurrected the molecule without the clinical program that would have answered the hard questions.

03How it might work

The albumin trick, step by step

The mechanism has two halves: the DAC half (how it survives) and the GHRH half (how it signals). Both are well understood in principle — what isn't understood is the long-term effect in healthy people.

The proposed chain: hitchhike, linger, dock, pulse. Each step is sound chemistry and biology — the unknowns start after the pulse.
Two halves, one trick. Think of this molecule as two inventions stuck together. The DAC tail handles survival — keeping the drug circulating for days. The GHRH-mimic head handles signaling — telling the pituitary to release growth hormone.

GHRH receptor activation

CJC-1295 is shaped like the brain's own GHRH signal. It fits the pituitary's GHRH receptor, the receptor fires its internal cascade, and stored growth hormone is released.

A mimic, not the real thing. CJC-1295 isn’t growth hormone, and it isn’t the brain’s own GHRH — it’s a synthetic lookalike shaped to fit the same receptor. A convincing shape is enough to trigger the receptor’s normal cascade. Whether a mimic reproduces every downstream effect of the natural signal is a separate question.

The DAC concept — a chemical hitchhiker

DAC (Drug Affinity Complex) is a small tail added to the peptide. Minutes after entering the bloodstream it latches onto albumin — a big, long-lived blood protein. Riding albumin, the drug dodges the kidneys' fast clearance and circulates for days instead of minutes.

Borrowing the body’s delivery truck. Albumin is the most abundant protein in blood plasma, and the body already uses it to ferry molecules around. The DAC tail’s maleimide group latches onto albumin within minutes of entering the bloodstream. Hitched to albumin, the drug inherits its slow clearance — days instead of minutes.

The GH pulse

When the receptor fires, stored growth hormone floods out in a pulse — a sharp rise, then a fall back toward baseline. That pulsing rhythm is how the body normally delivers GH.

GH → liver → IGF-1

Growth hormone itself is short-lived. Its main job is to reach the liver and order production of IGF-1 — the longer-lasting messenger that carries most of GH's effects to tissues.

The messenger that actually matters. Growth hormone itself clears from blood fast — most of its work is outsourced. The liver reads the GH signal and builds IGF-1, a longer-lived messenger that carries the growth message to tissues. That’s why researchers track IGF-1: it’s the readable footprint of a GH pulse.

Natural pulse vs prolonged stimulation

The body's own rhythm is spiky: brief GH bursts with quiet gaps between. With the DAC modification keeping the signal circulating, stimulation stretches into a raised plateau lasting days — a fundamentally different exposure pattern, and the reason researchers can't assume the effects match natural pulsing.

Pattern matters as much as amount. The body doesn’t drip GH steadily — it fires in bursts, mostly at night, with quiet gaps between. A drug that holds the signal high for days creates a fundamentally different exposure pattern. Researchers can’t assume a week-long plateau does what natural pulses do, even when the total signal looks similar.

Why the DAC matters

Same peptide, two fates. Without the DAC tail the kidneys filter it out within minutes. With the tail, it grabs albumin and the same molecule keeps working for days — that is the entire point of the modification.

Duration timeline

The DAC version's whole selling point is time: where the plain peptide is gone in minutes, the albumin-bound form keeps signaling across days. Researchers measured GH and IGF-1 elevation persisting well beyond a single day.

Receptor → hormone pathway

The full chain in one view: the receptor catches the mimic, the cell fires its internal signal, GH vesicles dump into the blood, the liver converts the message into IGF-1, and tissues respond. Every step downstream amplifies the last.

Plain English

The clever part is pharmacokinetics, not pharmacology: the signal itself is the same one your brain already uses. The innovation is making the signal linger — and 'lingering' is exactly where the unknowns live.

04Evidence check

What the science actually supports

There is real human data here — more than most research peptides get. There are no efficacy trials, no approvals, and no long-term studies. Both halves of that sentence matter.

Small human studies: yes

Early studies in people showed single injections raised growth hormone and kept IGF-1 elevated for days. That's genuine human pharmacology data — rare in this space.

Efficacy trials: none

No trial ever tested whether CJC-1295 with DAC treats or improves any condition. Showing a hormone moved is not the same as showing a benefit.

Long-term safety: blank

Nobody has studied months or years of sustained GH elevation with this drug. The program ended before those questions were asked.

Real short-term human data; nothing beyond that. The file is lopsided by design — the program stopped early.
Plain English

The honest summary: real human pharmacology, zero clinical proof. It demonstrably does the thing it was designed to do in the short term — and everything after that is extrapolation.

Expected physiological changes

When GH and IGF-1 rise and stay raised for days, the body's machinery shifts in predictable ways — at least in theory. Protein synthesis ticks up, fat metabolism leans toward breakdown, and cells get a louder "grow and repair" signal than they would from a brief natural pulse. In the short human studies, researchers could see the upstream numbers move: GH pulses got bigger and IGF-1 climbed. What they did not measure is whether those shifts turned into anything a person could feel or see — no body-composition scans over months, no strength tests, no metabolic ward data. The physiology is plausible because the hormones involved are well understood; the outcomes are unproven because the trials never ran long enough to look.

Research findings

What actually exists: small human pharmacology studies in the mid-2000s showing that a single administration raised GH and IGF-1 for an extended period — proof the DAC concept works in people, not just on paper. One study tracked GH pulsatility and found amplified pulses; another confirmed IGF-1 elevation lasting days. The program's sponsor then shelved development, so no efficacy trials (fat loss, muscle, anything clinical) were ever completed. Animal work exists but adds little beyond mechanism confirmation. In short: the delivery trick is validated, the therapeutic payoff was never tested.

Limitations

The evidence gaps are the story here. No long-term human data at all — nothing on what weeks or months of elevated GH/IGF-1 from this route does to insulin sensitivity, organ growth, or cancer risk markers. The prolonged-exposure pattern has no natural equivalent, so even the rich literature on natural GH pulses doesn't directly transfer. Dosing frequency in the old studies doesn't map to any modern protocol. And everything discussed online about results comes from uncontrolled self-experimentation with unregulated vials — no blinding, no controls, no verification of what's actually in the vial. Treat the entire topic as an unfinished experiment, not a settled one.

What it does

  • Mimics GHRH at the pituitary receptor
  • Amplifies GH pulses measurably in humans
  • Raises IGF-1 for days via the DAC tail
  • Demonstrates the albumin-riding concept works

What it doesn't do

  • Prove any fat-loss, muscle, or anti-aging effect
  • Replace natural pulsatility — it overrides it
  • Carry any long-term safety data
  • Have an approved medical use anywhere
05What people say

Reports from the wild

Almost everything online comes from the gray-market research-peptide scene — people buying unregulated vials and reporting back. These are stories, not data: vivid, unverified, and often about the wrong molecule entirely.

Unverified anecdote — the pulsing dot marks a paraphrased recurring theme from forums and social media: not verified, not evidence.

The weekly pin

“One shot and done for the week — that's the whole appeal.” Caveat: convenience says nothing about safety or efficacy; a nicer schedule isn't a better drug. Posters describe the appeal as almost entirely about the schedule: one administration and then forgetting about it for days, which feels closer to a normal routine than anything requiring frequent attention. Several mention choosing the DAC version specifically because they travel or dislike the ritual of regular pinning. A few note the flip side — that a long-acting compound also means a long-acting mistake if something feels off, since you can't just skip the next day's dose. The convenience narrative rarely mentions that trade-off.

Pattern: pharmacokinetics marketed as the feature, unknowns as footnotes.

Which CJC?

“Been on CJC for months” — but which version? Most can't say. Caveat: DAC and no-DAC are different drugs with different profiles; stories may describe the wrong one. Confusion between the DAC and no-DAC versions is one of the most common threads, with newcomers asking which one the old studies and the famous stack discussions actually refer to. Veterans reply that most community protocols and the synergy research people cite involve the short-acting form, not the DAC version. Several posters admit they bought one thinking it was the other, only realizing the difference when the effects — or lack of them — didn't match what they'd read. The naming overlap keeps generating the same mix-up year after year.

Pattern: the most common mix-up in the GHRH space.

My IGF-1 went up

“Bloodwork shows my IGF-1 doubled — it's working.” Caveat: higher IGF-1 proves exposure, not benefit; it's a marker, not an outcome. Bloodwork posts are the closest thing the community has to data, and IGF-1 numbers get shared like trophies. Posters typically report a rise over their baseline after some weeks, presented as proof the vial contains something active. Skeptics in the replies point out the obvious gaps: no control, no blinding, no way to verify the vial's contents or concentration, and natural IGF-1 varies with sleep, diet, and stress. A few careful posters include their full context — age, timing of the draw, what else they were taking — which at least makes the anecdote interpretable.

Pattern: surrogate markers presented as results.

Water and joints

“Hands puffy, joints achy, some tingling at night.” Caveat: these mirror GH-excess biology — but they're also common and nonspecific. A recurring theme is puffiness — rings feeling tight, ankles swelling slightly, a general sense of holding water. Some posters frame this as a sign the GH axis is engaged, since fluid retention is a known GH effect; others find it uncomfortable enough to stop. Joint aches, especially in hands and wrists, come up alongside the water talk, with carpal-tunnel-like tingling mentioned more than once. The replies usually split between 'that's the GH working' and 'that's your body telling you to stop,' with no way to settle it.

Pattern: side effects that match the drug's known mechanism.

Nothing happened

“Ran it for two months, felt absolutely no different.” Caveat: 'feeling' GH is unreliable — but quiet non-response is heavily underreported. Not every story is a success story — a fair number of posters report feeling absolutely nothing over weeks of use. Some conclude their vial was underdosed or fake; others wonder whether their expectations were simply unrealistic for a compound with no proven efficacy endpoint. A few note the uncomfortable possibility that nothing happening is exactly what you'd expect from an unproven research chemical. These reports get less attention than the dramatic ones, which probably skews the overall picture.

Pattern: selection bias toward dramatic stories.

Stacked with a GHRP

“Running it with a ghrelin mimetic — the synergy is unreal.” Caveat: two drugs, one story: attribution is impossible when they're always combined. The classic combination discussion centers on pairing the DAC version with a ghrelin-mimic peptide, on the theory that two pathways beat one. Posters describe the logic confidently but the reported outcomes are all over the map — some claim noticeably better results, others report mostly more side effects. Almost nobody in these threads seems to realize the synergy research they cite was done with the short-acting CJC form, not the DAC version. The mismatch between the cited science and the actual practice is rarely acknowledged.

Pattern: synergy claims with no way to isolate effects.

The long sleeper

“Sleep is deeper, recovery feels faster.” Caveat: GH affects sleep architecture — but self-reported sleep is highly placebo-prone. Sleep changes get mentioned often — deeper sleep, vivid dreams, waking up feeling more rested. GH is closely tied to deep sleep physiology, so posters tend to treat better sleep as confirmation the compound is doing something real. But sleep is also exquisitely sensitive to expectation, routine changes, and the simple fact of paying attention to it. Without any objective sleep tracking in these reports, it's impossible to separate a pharmacological effect from a very convincing placebo plus a new bedtime ritual.

Pattern: subjective outcomes dominate the reports.

Source roulette

“First vial was great, second from a new source did nothing.” Caveat: unregulated products vary wildly; different vials may contain different chemicals. Quality control is the ghost haunting every thread — posters openly discuss receiving vials that were clearly underdosed, mislabeled, or contaminated. Some describe doing their own crude checks; most just go by feel, which everyone admits is unreliable. A recurring piece of advice is that with unregulated products, you're testing the vendor as much as the compound. Several long-time posters say they've stopped entirely, not because of the pharmacology but because they no longer trust what's in the vial.

Pattern: anecdotes that can't be compared to each other.

Read these the way scientists read case reports: useful for generating hypotheses, useless for proving them. And with two molecules sharing one name, a good share of these stories may be about the wrong drug.

06Risks & unknowns

Risks & unknowns

With only small, short-term human studies, the risk picture is what's been observed, what's theoretically plausible, and what's simply unknown — kept in separate boxes on purpose.

Plain English

Known: tested athletes steer clear of it, and the vials are unregulated. Theoretical: week-long GH signaling nudges biology toward acromegaly-like territory. Unknown: almost everything else. That's not a risk profile — it's a risk outline with the details missing.

07Head to Head

CJC-1295 with DAC vs the field

The DAC version is constantly lumped in with every other GH-related compound — but duration is its whole identity. Here is how it actually differs from its relatives, one matchup at a time.

THE SIBLING

CJC-1295 with DAC vs CJC-1295 without DAC

MechanismIdentical GHRH-mimic core — the only difference is the DAC tail. No-DAC binds the same pituitary receptor the same way.
DurationWith DAC: active for days (albumin-bound). Without: cleared in roughly half an hour — it needs frequent re-dosing to keep working.
GH · IGF-1With DAC: a sustained raised plateau. Without: brief repeated spikes that fade fast between administrations.
EvidenceWith DAC: small human pharmacology studies from the 2000s, then the program was shelved. Without: even thinner — mostly community use, almost no formal data.
AdvantagesDAC version proved the long-acting concept in people. No-DAC version is the 'cleaner' experiment — closer to natural pulsing.
LimitationsDAC: no efficacy trials, prolonged exposure is uncharted. No-DAC: impractical duration, and still no real trials either.
Plain English: Same engine, different fuel tank. The DAC tail doesn't change what the drug does — it changes how long each dose keeps doing it.
THE GHRELIN COUSIN

CJC-1295 with DAC vs Ipamorelin

MechanismCJC-DAC copies GHRH and pushes the pituitary's 'release' button. Ipamorelin mimics ghrelin and knocks on a completely different receptor (GHS-R1a) to trigger a GH pulse.
DurationCJC-DAC: days per administration. Ipamorelin: short-acting, measured in hours — the pulse comes and goes.
GH · IGF-1CJC-DAC: prolonged elevation, less pulsatile. Ipamorelin: sharp selective pulses that preserve the natural rhythm better.
EvidenceBoth have small human datasets and discontinued programs. Ipamorelin's selectivity (less hunger, less cortisol spillover) is its documented edge.
AdvantagesCJC-DAC wins on duration; ipamorelin wins on selectivity and a more natural pulse shape.
LimitationsNeither has efficacy trials. CJC-DAC's long exposure raises unanswered questions; ipamorelin's short action needs repeat use.
Plain English: Different doors into the same building. One leans on the GHRH receptor for days; the other taps the ghrelin receptor for a quick, clean pulse.
THE CLASSIC STACK

CJC-1295 with DAC vs CJC + Ipamorelin

MechanismCJC-DAC alone: single GHRH-pathway push, stretched over days. The stack: no-DAC CJC (GHRH path) plus ipamorelin (ghrelin path) — two receptors, one coordinated pulse.
DurationCJC-DAC: one long plateau. The stack: short, synchronized pulses timed by the user — more control, more administrations.
GH · IGF-1The stack's rationale is synergy: hitting both receptors at once amplifies the pulse more than either alone. CJC-DAC achieves magnitude through duration instead.
EvidenceThe synergy claim comes from small studies of the two pathways combined — but with the no-DAC form, not the DAC form. Stacking the DAC version with ipamorelin is forum territory with no data.
AdvantagesStack: bigger pulses, both pathways, user-controlled timing. DAC alone: simplicity — one long-acting signal.
LimitationsThe famous stack results don't transfer to the DAC version. Combining a days-long signal with a pulse amplifier is mechanistically speculative.
Plain English: Two philosophies: one long river versus two synchronized waves. The stack's evidence belongs to the short-acting form — don't borrow it for the DAC version.
THE APPROVED ONE

CJC-1295 with DAC vs Tesamorelin

MechanismBoth are GHRH analogs — same receptor, same pituitary 'release' command. Tesamorelin is a shorter-acting GHRH copy; CJC-DAC is the same idea with an albumin-riding tail.
DurationTesamorelin: daily administration, effects measured day to day. CJC-DAC: days of activity per administration — the long-acting cousin that never made it.
GH · IGF-1Tesamorelin produces a steadier, studied elevation pattern. CJC-DAC's plateau is longer per dose but far less characterized.
EvidenceTesamorelin has phase-3 trials and an actual approval (HIV lipodystrophy) — the only one in this lineup with that. CJC-DAC has small pharmacology studies and a shelved program.
AdvantagesTesamorelin: real trials, real label, known safety profile. CJC-DAC: longer action per dose — in theory.
LimitationsTesamorelin's label is narrow (one indication). CJC-DAC has no efficacy data at all — duration without proof.
Plain English: Same family, opposite fates. Tesamorelin finished the marathon and got approved; CJC-DAC showed a clever trick in the lab and never ran the race.
THE ORIGINAL

CJC-1295 with DAC vs Sermorelin

MechanismSermorelin is a short 29-amino-acid GHRH fragment — the original 'go' mimic. CJC-DAC is a sturdier, modified GHRH analog with the albumin tail bolted on.
DurationSermorelin: very short-acting, gone quickly. CJC-DAC: days — the modification exists precisely to fix sermorelin's fleeting nature.
GH · IGF-1Sermorelin: brief pulse amplification, then gone. CJC-DAC: the same receptor push, stretched into a plateau.
EvidenceSermorelin has the longest history — it was once an actual medicine for GH-deficiency diagnosis. CJC-DAC never got past early studies.
AdvantagesSermorelin: well understood, historical clinical use. CJC-DAC: far longer action per dose.
LimitationsSermorelin was superseded and largely left practice. CJC-DAC's longevity is unproven in any clinical sense.
Plain English: Grandfather versus grandchild. Sermorelin proved the GHRH concept decades ago; CJC-DAC tried to make it last a week and stalled in development.
THE HORMONE ITSELF

CJC-1295 with DAC vs HGH

MechanismCJC-DAC nudges the pituitary to release the body's own GH. HGH (somatropin) skips all of that and supplies the hormone directly.
DurationCJC-DAC: days of pituitary stimulation per dose. HGH: the hormone itself, with its own pharmacokinetics — direct and precisely measurable.
GH · IGF-1CJC-DAC: indirect, pulsatile-ish elevation that still needs a working pituitary. HGH: direct, continuous elevation — no pulse, no feedback preserved.
EvidenceHGH has decades of clinical data, approved uses, and known trade-offs. CJC-DAC has early pharmacology studies and nothing more.
AdvantagesCJC-DAC: preserves some natural regulation (in theory). HGH: precise, studied, predictable.
LimitationsCJC-DAC only works if the pituitary cooperates, and long-term effects are unknown. HGH flattens natural feedback and carries the heaviest side-effect profile.
Plain English: Turning up the faucet versus pouring from the jug. One asks your pituitary to work overtime for days; the other replaces the whole system with a measured dose.
Plain English

The thread connecting every comparison: duration changes everything. A days-long GHRH signal is not just a longer version of a short pulse — it's a different exposure pattern, with different unknowns. None of the short-acting evidence transfers cleanly, and none of the approved drugs' safety data covers it either.

08Benefits

Potential benefits

Everything below is sorted into three evidence tiers on purpose — because “the hormone moved,” “biology predicts,” and “forums say” are not the same claim. Read the badges first.

Established · human data

GH and IGF-1 rose in early human studies

The one solid finding: in small short-term studies, a single administration measurably raised growth hormone and kept IGF-1 elevated for days. That is genuine human pharmacology — the DAC duration trick demonstrably works in people.

  • GH pulses amplified after a single administration
  • IGF-1 stayed raised for days, not hours
  • The albumin-binding concept verified in humans, not just on paper

Think of it this way: the speedometer moved — that proves the engine runs. It does not prove the trip was worth taking. A higher IGF-1 number is exposure, not a benefit.

Emerging / predicted

Body-composition interest — predicted, never proven

Sustained GH/IGF-1 signaling should, in theory, nudge fat metabolism and protein synthesis — but no trial of the DAC version ever measured body composition, fat loss, or muscle gain. These are predictions borrowed from general GH biology, not findings about this drug.

  • Theoretical: a louder “grow and repair” signal sustained over days
  • Theoretical: fat metabolism leaning toward breakdown
  • Untested: any of this in people taking CJC-1295 with DAC

Think of it this way: knowing a key fits a lock does not tell you what is inside the room. The biology predicts effects; the trials to confirm them never happened.

Community reports · stories, not data

What forums describe

Recurring forum themes — all unverified, often about the wrong molecule (DAC and no-DAC share a name), and tangled with expectation. Read as stories, not evidence.

  • “Bloodwork shows my IGF-1 went up” — the trophy post; proves exposure, not benefit
  • Deeper sleep and “faster recovery” — vivid, placebo-prone, uncontrolled
  • Puffiness and tingling — mirrors GH-excess biology, but also nonspecific
  • “Felt nothing at all” — the quiet non-responders, heavily underreported
09Who it suits

Who it may suit — and who it should not

There is no approved use for CJC-1295 with DAC — so this is not a patient guide. It is a map of who the research involved, and who should steer furthest clear of the gray-market scene around it.

May be relevant to

  • Readers studying GH-axis science — the DAC program is a real, if shelved, chapter of long-acting drug design
  • Healthy adults were the subjects of the small early trials — the only human population ever formally studied
  • Drug-design students — the albumin-binding trick is the same engineering idea used in approved long-acting medicines

May not suit / extra caution

  • Tested athletes — prohibited by WADA; a positive test ends careers regardless of the science debate
  • Anyone with blood-sugar or metabolic concerns — sustained GH signaling pushes against insulin sensitivity; unstudied for this drug
  • Anyone expecting proven benefits — no efficacy trial exists; hope is not a study result
  • Anyone wary of unknowns — a week-long exposure pattern with no long-term data is the opposite of a known quantity

11Takeaways

The bottom line

Ten chapters, distilled. What the molecule is, what the evidence says, and what would have to change.

🧬

A GHRH copy with a week-long tail

CJC-1295 with DAC mimics the brain's growth-hormone-release signal, with a maleimide tail that rides albumin for about a week. The maleimide tail's albumin handshake is genuinely clever drug engineering — the same class of trick used in approved long-acting medicines. But clever delivery is not the same as proven therapy: the tail solves the pharmacokinetics problem while leaving the efficacy and safety questions completely open. It's worth appreciating the design while remembering that design alone never made a medicine.

⏱️

Two molecules, one name

The no-DAC version lasts ~30 minutes. Mixing the two up — as forums constantly do — means mixing up two different drugs. This confusion has real consequences — people cite research on one version to justify using the other, and the famous stack synergy data belongs to the short-acting form. When reading any claim about 'CJC-1295,' the first question should always be: which one? The DAC version's claim to fame is duration; the no-DAC version's is fidelity to natural pulsing. They're different tools that happen to share a name.

🧪

Real short-term human data

Small early studies showed GH pulses and days of elevated IGF-1 from single injections. Rare, genuine human pharmacology for this space. These studies are the strongest thing the DAC version has going for it — actual human pharmacology, not just animal work or theory. Researchers watched GH pulses grow and IGF-1 stay elevated for days after a single administration, confirming the albumin trick works in people. But pharmacology is not therapy: showing a drug does what it was designed to do is step one of a very long staircase. The program stopped before step two.

📉

Program shelved over a decade ago

The developer discontinued development — no efficacy trials, no approvals, no long-term studies ever happened. Companies shelve programs for many reasons — business priorities, portfolio shifts, safety signals — and the public record doesn't always say which. What matters for anyone reading about it today is the consequence: no one picked up where the developer left off, so the evidence froze in the mid-2000s. A decade-plus gap means modern standards of evidence, manufacturing, and safety monitoring were never applied.

0️⃣

Zero clinical proof

No trial tested whether it helps any condition. Hormones moving on bloodwork is not the same as a benefit. This is the hardest limit on the entire topic. Hormone levels moving is a biomarker result; feeling better, losing fat, or gaining muscle are clinical results — and the bridge between them was never built. Every benefit attributed to the DAC version is extrapolated from the general GH literature or from uncontrolled anecdotes. Extrapolation is not evidence, no matter how plausible it sounds.

💬

Stories aren't data

Forum reports are vivid, unverified, and often about the wrong molecule — DAC and no-DAC share a name. The forum literature is genuinely interesting as a sociological phenomenon — hundreds of people running uncontrolled experiments and comparing notes. But without controls, blinding, verified product, or consistent measurement, the signal-to-noise ratio is terrible. The most dramatic reports get the most attention, the null results fade away, and nobody publishes their negative data. Read them as stories, which is what they are.

🩸

IGF-1 is a marker, not an outcome

Higher IGF-1 on bloodwork proves exposure, not that anything good or safe happened. This distinction is the single most important critical-thinking tool for this topic. A higher lab number feels like progress, and in a sense it is — it confirms the drug engaged its target. But medicine is full of drugs that moved biomarkers beautifully and failed to help patients, or even harmed them. Until someone runs the trial that connects the number to an outcome, the number is just a number.

⚠️

The lingering worry

Week-long GH signaling makes acromegaly-like effects (organ growth, insulin resistance) theoretically more plausible — unstudied. The concern isn't hypothetical hand-waving — chronically elevated GH signaling is exactly what goes wrong in acromegaly, a real disease with real consequences for organs, metabolism, and appearance. The DAC version's whole point is prolonged elevation, which is uncomfortably close to the pattern medicine worries about. Short studies can't detect slow-developing effects, and no long studies exist. This is the unknown that should bother anyone considering it most.
